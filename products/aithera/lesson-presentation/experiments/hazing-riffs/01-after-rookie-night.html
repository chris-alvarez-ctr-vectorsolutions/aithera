Where it can go

Five doors, and you can open any of them.

Each door is a different way to report hazing or get support. Some don’t need your name at all.

Always first

911 or campus emergency services

If anyone shows signs of significant distress, such as unconsciousness, difficulty breathing, or signs of alcohol poisoning, this door comes first.

It takes priority over the hotline, anonymous reporting, and every other channel.

Anonymous option

The National Anti-Hazing Hotline

Call 1-888-NOT-HAZE (1-888-668-4293). You can leave an anonymous message describing your concern, or leave your contact information if you want a direct response.

The law

Not being there isn’t a defense everywhere.

Hazing laws vary by state. At least one state holds hazing activity planners responsible for their behavior, even if they are not actively involved in the actual hazing act.

Planning a hazing event can result in criminal liability.

One incident. Three separate tracks.

Individuals found guilty of hazing can face criminal, civil, and academic consequences. Hazing is prosecutable under criminal law in most states, and civil suits and campus consequences are additional, separate tracks.

Hazing isn’t just a school conduct issue.

Try it

Misdemeanor or felony?

Not every state has an antihazing law, though hazing occurs in every state. Most states with antihazing laws classify hazing as a misdemeanor. A handful of states classify it as a felony if there is significant injury or death.

Misdemeanor

In most states with antihazing laws.

A team can lose more than one night.

Colleges can impose consequences on teams, clubs, or groups found responsible for hazing: forfeiting games, suspending the organization for a certain number of years, or eliminating it entirely.

Eliminated entirely

Why hazing gets counted

Hazing has gained more publicity and national attention since the late 1970s, tragically because of hazing deaths.

Institutions also report hazing incidents. Under SCHA Researched addition · Stop Campus Hazing Act · verify, the reporting requirement for Clery Act annual security reports mirrors the structure institutions use for Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) offenses.

The next season

Reporting isn’t snitching

Last year, you were Dana’s roommate. This year, there’s a new rookie class.

The team chat

The captains have planned a rookie initiation.

You know where this went last year. You also know what people call someone who reports it.

The worry

“Reporting would be snitching.”

Hazing causes real and lasting harm, and it can be deadly. Reporting it through available channels, including anonymously, protects your teammates. It’s consistent with genuine care for the people on your team.

The worry

“They’ll know it was me.”

You don’t need to reveal your identity to report. The National Anti-Hazing Hotline accepts anonymous messages, and most institutions have anonymous online reporting.

The worry

“I’ll get dragged into an investigation.”

Reporting through the hotline, anonymous online reporting, or confidential counseling doesn’t require you to take part in a formal investigation. Institutions use reports to protect students and address safety concerns, not to force the reporter into a drawn-out process.

The worry

“It won’t change anything.”

Reporting hazing helps organizations identify gaps and improve their policies and procedures. That reduces the chance that future members are harmed the same way.

Membership on a team should not require being injured or harmed.

Before you join anything

If you’re considering joining a club, group, or team, be aware of initiation rituals designed to humiliate, degrade, abuse, or endanger well-being, especially ones involving alcohol. Report them through the channels you know.

And choose to keep yourself and your teammates safe by not promoting or taking part in dangerous, illegal hazing.
