The headline

Three things came out of the comparison, in descending order of how much they should change what we do next.

Our module boundary is right. The re-composition's Outcome 4 — “Handle and Dispose of Sharps Safely” — is four objectives, one Know, two Feel, one Do, and four of our eight are near-verbatim matches for them. We independently landed on the same decomposition, which is the single most useful thing either document tells us.

The module has no “why.” It teaches a four-step procedure and never defines a sharp, never explains that a used needle keeps a trace of blood on it, and never names what that blood can carry. The words hepatitis, HBV, HCV and HIV appear nowhere in the file except the course title. Every other outcome in the re-composition carries a Know/Understand objective; sharps carries none — so this is a gap in their document too, and it is the same gap.

The re-composition gates recall and nothing else. Across all 26 objectives there are exactly six scored pass/fail checks, one per outcome, and every one is attached to a Recall (Know) objective. All four Feel objectives get an agreement scale with, in the document's own words, “no remediation, no gate.” All six Do objectives get a low-stakes multiple-choice question. A course whose premise is know–feel–do is, at the pass line, entirely know.

Worth leading the client conversation with

In the current course, nothing assesses sharps at all. The knowledge check that closes “Transmission and Risks” asks “Bloodborne pathogens ______” against the objective “Define the term ‘bloodborne pathogen’” — a definition from the previous section, which the earlier knowledge check already closed. So transmission, workplace risk, sharps and at-risk workers are taught across five slides and tested by none of them. That item is also [type: checkall] with three of four options correct and the same feedback paragraph on all four, so the learner reads identical text whether they were right or wrong.

Scope note for the client conversation. Their Outcome 4 is not the current Sharps slide. It is re-composed from at least four — Sharps, Engineering Controls, the Needlestick Incident scenario, and Personal Protective Equipment. The Chris-and-Jacob chain of harm that our whole coworker-responsibility case rests on comes from the PPE slide, not the sharps one. That is why sharps got bigger, and it means “the current sharps lesson” and “Outcome 4” are not the same scope.

Know content to add

All eight items below are drawn from the re-composition and are absent from the built module today. Every quotation is from the re-composed course document.

1. What a sharp is, and why a used one is dangerous

“A sharp is a medical needle or other sharp object that can puncture or cut the skin; a used medical needle is dangerous because it retains a small amount of blood after use and its extreme sharpness creates high probability of accidentally penetrating someone else's skin and exposing them to that retained blood.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.1, first teach point

This is the missing spine. Without it the four steps are arbitrary compliance, and the retained-blood mechanism is what makes the whole module's central image — a sharp at rest where somebody else will find it — mean anything.

2. What counts as a sharp besides a needle

“Cuts from contaminated sharps including broken glass, scalpels, exposed dental wires, and broken capillary tubes can transmit bloodborne pathogens.”

Outcome 1 · LO 1.4

This enumeration is what makes our four sectors legitimate rather than decorative: a box-cutter blade and contaminated glass are sharps by the source's own definition, not by our extension of it. The companion clause sits in the procedure itself — step one is to plan disposal “before using a needle or other sharp instrument or cleaning up broken glass” — and our procedure copy currently drops the broken-glass half, which is the only clause that carries the step into the three non-medical sectors.

3. What the next person can actually catch

“Needlestick injuries can result in exposure to bloodborne pathogens including HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C, which could result in severe health complications.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.2, first teach point

One sentence, once. Our four sector accounts land the consequence as procedure — “five months of testing,” “bloodwork every few weeks,” “post-exposure protocol for six months” — and never as the thing being tested for. This is also the only tie from Module 4 back to Module 1 and forward to the post-exposure module.

4. The transfer mechanism

“For bloodborne pathogens to pose a danger, they must transfer into your bloodstream.”

“A needlestick — unintentional being poked with a needle — transmits bloodborne pathogens the same way as deliberate needle sharing if the needle has been contaminated.”

Outcome 1 · LO 1.4

This is the reasoning content the module has none of: why the point matters, why the trace matters, and why a puncture is categorically different from the casual contact the course spends Outcome 1 ruling out.

5. The complete prohibition — five, not one

“In healthcare settings, used needles must not be bent, broken, recapped, removed, or separated from syringes; they must be placed in a sharps disposal container immediately or as soon as possible after use.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.1 and LO 4.4

The module currently says “recapping is the one absolute—every other rule here has an ‘unless’; that one does not” and builds a scored entry item on the claim. That is five absolutes compressed into one, and the copy has to change in both places it appears.

6. The no-container-available rule, stated plainly

“If no sharps container is immediately available, do not place the needle in general waste. Use the device's safety feature if available, seek a proper sharps container before disposal, and never use improvised soft-material wrapping.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.4, misconception correction

Our overfilled-container case circles this across three answer options without ever stating it. Stating the rule is also what lets the harder variant of that case be genuinely arguable instead of merely ambiguous.

7. What a designated container actually is

“Contaminated sharps and other contaminated waste must be handled and disposed of as regulated waste in closeable, leak-proof containers that are colored red or marked with the universal biohazard symbol.”

“Safety-engineered sharps disposal containers are designed to encapsulate sharp objects upon disposal.”

Outcome 3 · LO 3.3 and LO 3.2

The module says “designated container” more than twenty times and never once describes one. A learner on a renovation site or a plant floor has no idea what they are looking for — which is precisely the population the module claims to serve.

8. The correction on gloves

“Heavy-duty protective gloves made of materials like nitrile, latex, vinyl, or neoprene can protect hands from cuts, punctures, and exposure to hazardous substances, but PPE alone is less effective than engineering controls at preventing needlestick injuries.”

Misconception corrected: “Wearing heavy-duty gloves is a reliable substitute for proper sharps disposal containers. → Even thick gloves can be penetrated by needles.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.3

All four of our sector accounts already end with the needle going through a glove. The argument is dramatized and never named. This is the factual backing for the controls-versus-shortcuts beat described below.

Where the added content lands

Items 1, 3 and 4 become one new objective — a Know/Understand objective, the type sharps is missing and every other outcome has: “Explain why a used sharp is dangerous — it retains a trace of blood and its point breaks skin — and what that exposes the next person to.” Flag it to add instruction on a weak answer rather than to gate.

Items 2, 5, 6 and 7 are procedural detail on objectives we already have — they belong in the procedure beat and the recognition cases, not in new objectives. Item 8 backs the controls-versus-shortcuts beat.

This takes the module from eight objectives to nine, which strengthens the compression story rather than diluting it: reasoning content is the most defensible thing in the module to let a learner test out of, and right now the procedure is the only thing a strong entry result can buy.

Where we are ungrounded

Three claims the built module makes that the source material does not support as written.

Revised call — the overfilled container can be saved Changed

My first read was that this case had no support anywhere in the 33 pages. The specific claim — a fill line, a container packed above it, pressing contents down to make room — still has none: no occurrence of “fill line,” “overfilled,” or anything equivalent. But the principle the case needs is signed, in Outcome 3:

“Engineering controls must be inspected and maintained or replaced on a regular basis.”

Misconception corrected: “Engineering controls, once installed, require no ongoing attention.”

“Safety-engineered sharps disposal containers are designed to encapsulate sharp objects upon disposal.”

Outcome 3 · LO 3.2

A container above its fill line is an engineering control that has stopped encapsulating — which is exactly the misconception LO 3.2 names. So the case does not need cutting; it needs re-basing on that principle, with the fill-line specifics sourced from the customer's own exposure control plan rather than asserted by us. That also removes the awkwardness of the module's most memorable screen being its least defensible one.

The cohort survey has no source

Label or source

js/layered-sharps.js — DEBRIEF_DATA, and the “214 responses · representative data” caption

Neither document contains any norm or cohort data at all, so the shift-norms objective is entirely our construct. That is fine for a prototype, with one catch: here the number is the intervention. A norm-correction beat whose figure is invented is teaching something we made up. It needs either a real source or a visible “illustrative” label.

The regulatory citation is asserted, not checked

Verify

js/layered-sharps.js — the case-2 reviewer caption

We cite 1910.1030(g)(2)(vii)(E) on a reviewer-facing caption as the basis for content-locking the recognition objective. The paragraph most likely does support it — it is the training element covering recognition of tasks that may involve exposure — but that has not been read against the regulation here, and we are stating it as settled fact in a document a customer may see.

Defects found in the built module

Six, ordered by how visible they are to a reviewer. All were found by diffing the module against the two documents rather than by testing it.

1. The record reports an answer that was never collected

Bug

js/layered-sharps.js:1471

The entry battery asks four items: two on the procedure, one on coworker responsibility, one on shift norms. The controls-versus-shortcuts objective is asked nowhere in the module — and the record screen prints “Recorded · From your own answer · Noted and passed on” for it anyway. On a screen whose entire argument is that every line points at a real moment, this is the worst possible line to have invented.

Fix: build the beat, using the scenario the source already wrote for it — a facility decides between cut-resistant gloves for every waste handler, or more sharps containers throughout the building. Grounded, and it closes the hole.

2. One sector's norm figure inverts the beat's own message

Bug

js/layered-sharps.js:1169 and :1226

The construction sector is set at 48% “use the container immediately” — so a majority don't. The copy then tells the learner the real figure is “higher than most people guess” and that “the person who sets one down is the outlier, not the norm,” and the “most”-answer response congratulates them on a match. The highlight rule keys off 50% or better, so nothing highlights either.

Fix: either raise that sector above 50, or branch the copy so a minority figure reads as the finding it actually is. A norms intervention that argues against its own data is worse than no norms intervention.

3. Two Do objectives are scored from one button press

Bug

js/layered-sharps.js:1478 and :1483

The timed moment captures a single decision and its latency. The record then reports both the act-while-the-moment-is-open objective and the do-it-in-context objective from it, with the second one claiming “timing, technique, route and recovery all held.” There was no route and no recovery. There was one button.

Fix: either build the remaining simulation beats — the walked disposal, the adaptive complication, the open close — so the second objective has evidence of its own, or merge the two into one objective and report one result.

4. Two Feel objectives promise a post-measure that does not exist

Bug

js/layered-sharps.js:59 and :65

Both are marked as measured “Pre + post.” Both are asked once, at entry. Nothing re-asks them.

Fix: build the post-measure rather than downgrading the metadata — see the mapping recommendations below, where this turns out to be the most differentiating thing available to us.

5. The sector premise field is dead and wrong

Cleanup

js/layered-sharps.js:112 and :154

Every sector carries a premise flag distinguishing “you found somebody else's sharp” from “you used one yourself,” and the comment above it explains that this is two premise families rather than four coats of paint. The field is never read anywhere. It is also wrong for two of the four sectors: the education lens opens with the learner performing a finger-stick and the manufacturing lens with the learner changing a blade — both are use-premises by their own case text and their own simulation premise. Only the construction lens is genuinely a find. The source backs the use reading: its central manufacturing character is a worker who self-administers insulin.

Fix: correct the two flags and read the field somewhere, or delete it and the comment.

6. An objective clause that is taught and never assessed

Cleanup

js/layered-sharps.js — K1 objective text and the procedure beat

“Use needle alternatives when possible” is in the objective statement, taught as step two of the procedure, and assessed nowhere in the module. It is also meaningless in the construction lens — there is no alternative to a syringe you pulled out of a wall cavity.

Fix: assess it with a sector-gated item, or drop it from the objective statement and keep it as teach-only content. Right now it is in the signed objective and unevidenced, which is the one combination the record screen exists to make impossible.

Mapping more honestly to know–feel–do

Five changes. The first two are corrections; the last three are where the prototype has something the re-composition structurally cannot produce.

Reconcile the two vocabularies, or declare the divergence

The re-composition uses six types: Recall and Understand for Know, Believe and Value for Feel, Perform and Respond-in-the-moment for Do. We use eight sub-scales: Remember and Observe, Believe and Value and Perceive, Activate and Apply and Sustain. Nobody reading both can tell whether we diverged on purpose. Rename where we overlap — Remember to Recall, Apply to Perform, Activate to Respond-in-the-moment — and label Observe, Perceive and Sustain as declared extensions, each with a line of justification.

Add the Know/Understand objective

Covered above under Know content. It is the largest structural gap on either side, and it is what makes the coworker-harm account land clinically rather than only emotionally.

Build the Feel post-measure

Their attitude checks are one-shot, with — explicitly — no remediation and no gate. A pre/post pair around the coworker-harm account and the norms beat is something their pipeline cannot express at all, and the shift is the reportable number, not the level. This is the most defensible piece of Feel evidence available to us, and it retires defect 4.

Resolve the attitude-check conflict as a stated decision

Their specification says attitude checks carry no remediation and no gate. We flag both Feel objectives in the entry battery to add or harden a beat on a weak answer. I would keep ours and record their line as a limitation of their pipeline rather than a design rule — but it has to be written down, because at the moment our build silently contradicts the specification that is supposed to sit upstream of it.

Make the gate contrast explicit in the reviewer captions

Their six pass/fail checks are all on recall. Ours gates a Do objective and marks three Do objectives as never skippable. That is the strongest single argument the prototype makes about the re-composition's output, and it is currently only legible to somebody who has read both documents side by side.

Flags in the source documents

Nine things that do not hold up, in the order I would raise them.

  • Every gate is a recall gate. 26 objectives; 16 Know, 4 Feel, 6 Do. Six scored pass/fail checks, one per outcome, all on Recall (Know). Four attitude scales with no gate and no remediation by design. Six low-stakes multiple-choice questions for the Do objectives. Nothing about feeling or doing can affect whether a learner passes.
  • Every Do objective is assessed by multiple choice — including the sharps one, typed Perform (Do) and assessed with a single question. Meanwhile two other outcomes each flagged a “designed gap” on the grounds that their content was “not a performable sequence” and needed an authored scenario. The sharps Do objective is no more performable in a multiple-choice item than those were. Either the gap standard applies to it too, or the two flagged gaps did not need flagging.
  • Outcome 4's prerequisite is wrong, and it decides module order. It declares that it builds on Outcome 2, universal precautions. But its Value objective is entirely engineering-controls-versus-PPE — which is Outcome 3's content. Sharps needs Outcome 3, not just Outcome 2.
  • The post-exposure outcome diagnoses an attitude barrier and then assigns no Feel objectives. Its own text says workers “may freeze, minimize, or delay because the moment is frightening,” and its misconceptions are pure shame and paperwork avoidance — “better to handle it quietly without reporting.” That is a stronger attitude signal than the sharps outcome's, and the sharps outcome got two Feel objectives from it. Post-exposure reporting should have a Believe objective.
  • The controls Value objective measures knowledge. Its agreement statement is an empirical claim — that containers and safety-engineered needles prevent more injuries than PPE or training alone — rated one to five with no remediation, so a learner who disagrees with a true fact is never corrected. And its practice activity, choosing between gloves and containers, is a decision task, which is Do, not Feel. Our version inherits this verbatim, so fixing defect 1 is the chance to fix it properly: make the choice the evidence and the scale the self-report.
  • Pipeline identifiers leaked into a review document. “TB” is used without ever being expanded (“a foundational knowledge TB” — presumably target behavior), alongside content references like ref a8a06f6f and cl-0091/92/93 and the line “Verify_grounding confirmed all three elements fail the performance lens.” Nobody reviewing this can act on any of it.
  • Two referenced files were not provided. The document opens by telling the reader to “read LED Instructions in this folder — it explains how to give feedback and defines every field below (and how each traces back to the Know–Feel–Do model),” and says the matching source transcript is in the same folder. Without the instructions, the field definitions and the know–feel–do trace are asserted rather than checkable. Without the transcript we cannot spot-check grounding — which is exactly what the fill-line question needed. Worth asking for both.
  • The slide numbering disagrees. The storyboard excerpt labels Sharps as P012 and the knowledge check as P013; the course outline has Sharps at P011 with Workers Most At Risk at P012. One of the two is off by one, and it matters as soon as we trace objectives back to slides.
  • Nothing in the current course assesses sharps. Detailed at the top — the section-closing knowledge check tests a definition from the previous section, and gives identical feedback for right and wrong answers. This is the strongest available argument for the re-composition and should be presented as one.

Sequenced plan

Ordered so that each step either closes a defect or unblocks the next one. Steps 1–4 are the content pass; 5–7 are cleanup.

  1. Build the controls-versus-shortcuts beat. Closes the invented record line, plugs the one hole in the Feel triad, and takes a grounded scenario straight from the source. Assess the choice, then ask the agreement item — which fixes the type mismatch our version inherited.
  2. Add the Know/Understand objective. Definition of a sharp, the retained-blood mechanism, the transfer rule, and the three pathogens named once. Largest real gap, and a gap in their document too.
  3. Re-base the overfilled-container case on the maintenance principle. A control that is not maintained stops being a control. Fill-line specifics come from the customer's exposure control plan, not from us.
  4. Pay the copy debts. Five prohibitions rather than one; broken glass restored to step one; the not-just-healthcare sentence; the no-container rule stated; a designated container actually described.
  5. Build the Feel post-measure and retire the “Pre + post” promise the module does not currently keep. Report the shift, not the level.
  6. Fix the sector norm figure, and split or merge the two Do objectives. Both are cases of the record claiming more than the interaction produced.
  7. Rename the sub-scales to their vocabulary, declare our three extensions, and clean up the dead premise field.
