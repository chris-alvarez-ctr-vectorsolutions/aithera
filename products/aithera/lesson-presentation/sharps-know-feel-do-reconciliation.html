What the module has

A Know/Understand premise beat (K2, “Why It Is Dangerous”); a controls-versus-shortcuts beat (F2, “Gloves or Containers”); pre/post measurement on the two movable Feel objectives (F1, F3); separate evidence for both Do objectives from the culminating scenario; the overfilled-container case re-based on the maintenance principle; sub-scales named in the re-composition's vocabulary with four declared extensions; and a sector premise flag that the procedure actually reads.

Outstanding: the K2 hazard clip exists as a file but stays gated behind a review-only flag pending sign-off, so learners see the written version by default. The cohort figures are ours, not sourced, and labelled illustrative on the screen.

The headline

Three things came out of the comparison, in descending order of how much they should change what we do next.

The module boundary is right. The re-composition's Outcome 4 — “Handle and Dispose of Sharps Safely” — is four objectives, one Know, two Feel, one Do, and four of ours are near-verbatim matches for them. We independently landed on the same decomposition, which is the single most useful thing either document tells us.

Sharps has a “why.” K2 explains why a used sharp is dangerous: it retains a trace of blood after use, its point breaks skin, and a pathogen only has to reach a bloodstream to infect. Every other outcome in the re-composition carries a Know/Understand objective; sharps was the one exception in both documents, which is why K2 exists as our own objective rather than a straight port of theirs.

The re-composition gates recall and nothing else. Across all 26 objectives there are exactly six scored pass/fail checks, one per outcome, and every one is attached to a Recall (Know) objective. All four Feel objectives get an agreement scale with, in the document's own words, “no remediation, no gate.” All six Do objectives get a low-stakes multiple-choice question. A course whose premise is know–feel–do is, at the pass line, entirely know.

Worth leading the client conversation with

In the current course, nothing assesses sharps at all. The knowledge check that closes “Transmission and Risks” asks “Bloodborne pathogens ______” against the objective “Define the term ‘bloodborne pathogen’” — a definition from the previous section, which the earlier knowledge check already closed. So transmission, workplace risk, sharps and at-risk workers are taught across five slides and tested by none of them. That item is also [type: checkall] with three of four options correct and the same feedback paragraph on all four, so the learner reads identical text whether they were right or wrong.

Scope note for the client conversation. Their Outcome 4 is not the current Sharps slide. It is re-composed from at least four — Sharps, Engineering Controls, the Needlestick Incident scenario, and Personal Protective Equipment. The Chris-and-Jacob chain of harm that our whole coworker-responsibility case rests on comes from the PPE slide, not the sharps one. That is why sharps got bigger, and it means “the current sharps lesson” and “Outcome 4” are not the same scope.

K3's content-lock is grounded outside either document. The recognition objective is content-locked on 29 CFR 1910.1030(g)(2)(vii)(E), which requires training to include “an explanation of the appropriate methods for recognizing tasks and other activities that may involve exposure to blood and other potentially infectious materials” — a direct match for the objective's own text, not an adjacent or borrowed one.

Where the Know content is sourced

K2 and several procedural details in the safe-handling steps and recognition cases are built from these eight passages in the re-composition. Every quotation is from that document.

1. What a sharp is, and why a used one is dangerous

“A sharp is a medical needle or other sharp object that can puncture or cut the skin; a used medical needle is dangerous because it retains a small amount of blood after use and its extreme sharpness creates high probability of accidentally penetrating someone else's skin and exposing them to that retained blood.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.1, first teach point

This is K2's spine: without it the four-step procedure would be arbitrary compliance, and the retained-blood mechanism is what makes the module's central image — a sharp at rest where somebody else will find it — mean anything.

2. What counts as a sharp besides a needle

“Cuts from contaminated sharps including broken glass, scalpels, exposed dental wires, and broken capillary tubes can transmit bloodborne pathogens.”

Outcome 1 · LO 1.4

This enumeration is what makes the module's four sectors legitimate rather than decorative: a box-cutter blade and contaminated glass are sharps by the source's own definition. The companion clause sits in the procedure itself — step one is to plan disposal “before using a needle or other sharp instrument or cleaning up broken glass” — and the procedure copy carries the broken-glass half, which is what carries the step into the three non-medical sectors.

3. What the next person can actually catch

“Needlestick injuries can result in exposure to bloodborne pathogens including HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C, which could result in severe health complications.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.2, first teach point

One sentence, once, inside K2. The four sector accounts land the consequence as procedure — “five months of testing,” “bloodwork every few weeks,” “post-exposure protocol for six months” — and K2 is the only place the thing actually being tested for is named. It is also the only tie from Module 4 back to Module 1 and forward to the post-exposure module.

4. The transfer mechanism

“For bloodborne pathogens to pose a danger, they must transfer into your bloodstream.”

“A needlestick — unintentional being poked with a needle — transmits bloodborne pathogens the same way as deliberate needle sharing if the needle has been contaminated.”

Outcome 1 · LO 1.4

This is the reasoning K2 supplies: why the point matters, why the trace matters, and why a puncture is categorically different from the casual contact Outcome 1 spends its own content ruling out.

5. The complete prohibition — five, not one

“In healthcare settings, used needles must not be bent, broken, recapped, removed, or separated from syringes; they must be placed in a sharps disposal container immediately or as soon as possible after use.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.1 and LO 4.4

The module states all five, not one, in both places it appears — the procedure detail and CLARA's record-screen reply.

6. The no-container-available rule, stated plainly

“If no sharps container is immediately available, do not place the needle in general waste. Use the device's safety feature if available, seek a proper sharps container before disposal, and never use improvised soft-material wrapping.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.4, misconception correction

The overfilled-container case turns on this rule, which the safe-handling procedure copy now states directly rather than leaving implicit across answer options.

7. What a designated container actually is

“Contaminated sharps and other contaminated waste must be handled and disposed of as regulated waste in closeable, leak-proof containers that are colored red or marked with the universal biohazard symbol.”

“Safety-engineered sharps disposal containers are designed to encapsulate sharp objects upon disposal.”

Outcome 3 · LO 3.3 and LO 3.2

The safe-handling procedure describes one explicitly: rigid, closeable, leak-proof, and red or biohazard-marked — built to swallow the point so nothing can reach it again.

8. The correction on gloves

“Heavy-duty protective gloves made of materials like nitrile, latex, vinyl, or neoprene can protect hands from cuts, punctures, and exposure to hazardous substances, but PPE alone is less effective than engineering controls at preventing needlestick injuries.”

Misconception corrected: “Wearing heavy-duty gloves is a reliable substitute for proper sharps disposal containers. → Even thick gloves can be penetrated by needles.”

Outcome 4 · LO 4.3

This is the factual backing for the Gloves or Containers beat (F2): gloves resist a dragging blade, not a concentrated point, which is why every sector account ends with the needle going through one.

Where this content lives

Items 1, 3 and 4 are K2, the module's Know/Understand objective: “Explain why a used sharp is dangerous — it retains a trace of blood and its point breaks skin — and what that exposes the next person to.”

Items 2, 5, 6 and 7 are procedural detail carried in the safe-handling steps and the recognition cases, not separate objectives. Item 8 backs the Gloves or Containers beat (F2).

Where we are ungrounded

Two claims the built module makes that the source material does not support as written.

The overfilled-container case rests on an inferred principle

The specific claim — a fill line, a container packed above it, pressing contents down to make room — has no verbatim match anywhere in the 33 pages: no occurrence of “fill line,” “overfilled,” or anything equivalent. The case rests on the underlying principle instead, which is signed in Outcome 3:

“Engineering controls must be inspected and maintained or replaced on a regular basis.”

Misconception corrected: “Engineering controls, once installed, require no ongoing attention.”

“Safety-engineered sharps disposal containers are designed to encapsulate sharp objects upon disposal.”

Outcome 3 · LO 3.2

A container above its fill line is an engineering control that has stopped encapsulating — exactly the misconception LO 3.2 names. The specific fill-line details in each sector's scene are invented for the prototype: three-quarters full is the common manufacturer convention for a sharps container's fill line, not a fabricated fraction, but it is not sourced from any customer's actual exposure control plan. The correct answer deliberately never states a number — “set by your site's plan, not by eye” — so a real deployment can swap in a customer's own container spec without touching the lesson.

The cohort figures have no source

js/layered-sharps.js — the sector debrief data and the F3 cohort-debrief caption

Neither document contains any norm or cohort data at all, so the shift-norms and self-efficacy objectives are entirely our construct. Because the number is the intervention here, the figures are labelled illustrative on the learner-facing screen, with a note that a real deployment substitutes the customer's own cohort data.

Mapping honestly to know–feel–do

Five places the module's design is not a straight port of the re-composition's, and why.

The two vocabularies are reconciled

The re-composition uses six types: Recall and Understand for Know, Believe and Value for Feel, Perform and Respond-in-the-moment for Do. Sub-scales are named in the re-composition's vocabulary everywhere the two overlap — Recall, Understand, Believe, Value, Perform, Respond in the moment — with Observe, Perceive, Can and Sustain labelled as declared extensions, each carrying its own line of justification in code for why it exists beyond the source material.

K2 supplies the Know/Understand objective

Covered above under “Where the Know content is sourced.” It is the largest structural gap on either side, and it is what makes the coworker-harm account land clinically rather than only emotionally.

The Feel post-measure

Their attitude checks are one-shot, with — explicitly — no remediation and no gate. F1 and F3 both carry a post-measure, so the reportable number is the shift in a learner's answer, not the one-time level — something their pipeline cannot express at all. It is the most defensible piece of Feel evidence in the module.

The attitude-check divergence is a stated decision

Their specification says attitude checks carry no remediation and no gate. F1 and F3 both route a weak answer into an added or hardened beat instead. That is a deliberate divergence: their line reads as a limitation of their pipeline, not a design rule worth inheriting.

The gate contrast

Their six pass/fail checks are all on recall. The module gates two Do objectives (D1, D2) and marks all three Do objectives — D1, D2 and D3 — as never skipped. That is the strongest single argument the module makes about the re-composition's own assessment design, legible only to someone who has read both documents side by side — which is what the reviewer captions are for.

Flags in the source documents

Nine things that do not hold up, in the order I would raise them.

  • Every gate is a recall gate. 26 objectives; 16 Know, 4 Feel, 6 Do. Six scored pass/fail checks, one per outcome, all on Recall (Know). Four attitude scales with no gate and no remediation by design. Six low-stakes multiple-choice questions for the Do objectives. Nothing about feeling or doing can affect whether a learner passes.
  • Every Do objective is assessed by multiple choice — including the sharps one, typed Perform (Do) and assessed with a single question. Meanwhile two other outcomes each flagged a “designed gap” on the grounds that their content was “not a performable sequence” and needed an authored scenario. The sharps Do objective is no more performable in a multiple-choice item than those were. Either the gap standard applies to it too, or the two flagged gaps did not need flagging.
  • Outcome 4's prerequisite is wrong, and it decides module order. It declares that it builds on Outcome 2, universal precautions. But its Value objective is entirely engineering-controls-versus-PPE — which is Outcome 3's content. Sharps needs Outcome 3, not just Outcome 2.
  • The post-exposure outcome diagnoses an attitude barrier and then assigns no Feel objectives. Its own text says workers “may freeze, minimize, or delay because the moment is frightening,” and its misconceptions are pure shame and paperwork avoidance — “better to handle it quietly without reporting.” That is a stronger attitude signal than the sharps outcome's, and the sharps outcome got two Feel objectives from it. Post-exposure reporting should have a Believe objective.
  • The controls Value objective measures knowledge. Its agreement statement is an empirical claim — that containers and safety-engineered needles prevent more injuries than PPE or training alone — rated one to five with no remediation, so a learner who disagrees with a true fact is never corrected. And its practice activity, choosing between gloves and containers, is a decision task, which is Do, not Feel. F2 (the Gloves or Containers beat) fixes it: the choice between gloves and containers is the Do-shaped evidence, and the agreement scale is the self-report layered on top of it, rather than one item standing in for both.
  • Pipeline identifiers leaked into a review document. “TB” is used without ever being expanded (“a foundational knowledge TB” — presumably target behavior), alongside content references like ref a8a06f6f and cl-0091/92/93 and the line “Verify_grounding confirmed all three elements fail the performance lens.” Nobody reviewing this can act on any of it.
  • Two referenced files were not provided. The document opens by telling the reader to “read LED Instructions in this folder — it explains how to give feedback and defines every field below (and how each traces back to the Know–Feel–Do model),” and says the matching source transcript is in the same folder. Without the instructions, the field definitions and the know–feel–do trace are asserted rather than checkable. Without the transcript we cannot spot-check grounding — which is exactly what the fill-line question needed. Worth asking for both.
  • The slide numbering disagrees. The storyboard excerpt labels Sharps as P012 and the knowledge check as P013; the course outline has Sharps at P011 with Workers Most At Risk at P012. One of the two is off by one, and it matters as soon as we trace objectives back to slides.
  • Nothing in the current course assesses sharps. Detailed at the top — the section-closing knowledge check tests a definition from the previous section, and gives identical feedback for right and wrong answers. This is the strongest available argument for the re-composition and should be presented as one.
