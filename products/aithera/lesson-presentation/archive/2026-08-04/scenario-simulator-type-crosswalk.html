Scenario Simulator · Reconciliation

Two vocabularies, one simulator

The content team's “interaction types” and our engineering “scenario types” describe the same system at two different altitudes. This page lines them up side by side, shows that all five “final” frameworks are already built, and flags the name collisions that make the two vocabularies look like they disagree when they don't.

Companion to the Build & Authoring Spec (end-to-end flow) and The Interaction Layer (modes & prompts).

1

The core idea: two layers, not two lists

The confusion is that both teams say “type,” but they mean different layers. The content mapping names what the learner does in a single beat. Our build names the whole-scenario engine that sequences those beats. Neither is wrong — they stack.

Layer A · the beat — “interaction type” (content vocabulary)

What the learner is doing at one input moment

This is the content team's mapping. In our runtime it already exists: it's the Engage type on the interaction skeleton, and on our multi-phase engines it's a literal per-phase field — world: 'scene' | 'coaching'.

Roleplay talk to a character Coach-Led Inquiry reason with the coach Observe / React read a stimulus

“Mix & Match” is not a fourth beat type — it's what you get when one scenario uses several of the above across its phases.

Layer B · the engine — “scenario type” (our vocabulary)

The whole-scenario pedagogy that strings beats together

Seven ship today. Four are effectively single-beat; three are multi-phase sequencers — and those three are “Mix & Match,” built as engines.

Single-beat engines — the scenario is one interaction type throughout
Roleplay action-practice Footage Observe/React observe-react Teach-Back teach-back Spot-the-Scene scene-sweep
Multi-phase sequencers — mix beat types across an ordered arc ( = “Mix & Match”)
Guided Arc guided-arc Branching Arc branching-arc Ensemble Arc ensemble-arc

Read it this way: a content author picks interaction types beat by beat; a developer picks the engine that can run that sequence. The mapping between them is the rest of this page.

4

Name collisions — the landmines

These are the terms that mean different things to the two teams. Every one is a place where handing a document straight across would point someone at the wrong build. Resolve these first.

Observe / React the big one

Content meaning: spot issues in a static illustrated image (HazCom / Spot the Hazard).
Our code meaning: observe-react.js = watch video footage and react (the HazMat tanker call) — a different pedagogy entirely.

The content team's “Observe/React” is our scene-sweep, not our observe-react. Send this doc to eng as-is and they reach for the wrong module.

HazCom vs HazMat

One letter apart, and they currently point at different builds. HazCom = the image-spotting scenario (our scene-sweep). HazMat = the footage scenario (our observe-react). Our own interaction-layer doc even used “HazCom” as a Teach-Back illustration — a third pin on the same word.

Pick one label per build and use it everywhere. Easiest fix in the set.

Coach-Led Inquiry a type on one side, a beat on the other

Content meaning: a top-level interaction type — in fact the most common one. Our build: not a type at all; a beat that lives inside the sequencer engines, with Teach-Back as its one packaged standalone flavor.

Agree it's a beat-level primitive. Then decide whether it deserves a first-class authorable engine (§5, gaps).

Mix & Match a fourth type, or the default?

Content meaning: listed alongside the three real types as if it were a build target. Our build: the ordinary behavior of any multi-phase engine — nothing to build, nothing to pick.

Drop it from the list of types; describe it as “a scenario spans multiple interaction types,” which the sequencers already do.
5

What each side should take from this

The content mapping is right about the beat layer — and it surfaces two things our build genuinely hasn't settled. Both columns are action items.

For the content mapping

Three things it doesn't yet reflect

  • All five frameworks already run. Reframe the doc from “types these will need” to “the interaction types each shipped prototype uses.”
  • “Mix & Match” is the default, handled by the sequencer engines — not a fourth type to define.
  • The intro video isn't an untyped loose end. It's the context-intro modality (video / story / audio / reading / none) — a solved, swappable first part, which answers the “video landings” open item.

And the distinctions the beat taxonomy flattens but our build keeps: Practice⇄Learn alternation, hard/soft action-gates (the required move), Teach-Back as an inverted produce-and-grade mode, and the standardized debrief + proficiency record.

For our build

Real gaps the mapping exposes

  • Our own spec is stale. The interaction-layer doc described four modes; seven ship. It's now refreshed to all seven — but that lag is what let the vocabularies drift.
  • Beat-composition may be the better primitive. “An experience = a sequence of phases, each one interaction type” is a cleaner authoring contract than “pick one of seven bespoke engines.” Worth weighing for productionization.
  • No first-class Coach-Led Inquiry. The workhorse beat has no simple authorable home; a plain reason-with-the-coach scenario is awkward to build today.
  • The open items are open for us too: do Learn-mode input beats count as practice; is reacting to an artifact (a threat screenshot) Observe/React or Coach-Led; should the Bullying hallway become a second perception beat.
The one-line reconciliation: the content team names the beats, we name the engines that sequence them, and the two only look like different taxonomies because “type,” “Observe/React,” and “HazCom” each carry two meanings. Fix the words and the disagreement disappears.
