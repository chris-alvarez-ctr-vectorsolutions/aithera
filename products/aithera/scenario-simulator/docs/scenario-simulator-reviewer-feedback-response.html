The shape of it

One failure family, and it's an architecture question

Themes 1, 3, 4, 9 and 10 are the same defect wearing five hats: the debrief fires unconditionally, so when the learner gives it nothing, the coach invents something to praise. The audit's own root-cause table says it plainly — the identical affirmation appears verbatim against gibberish, hostility, and injection attempts. That single family carries the large majority of 1★ ratings.

The remaining findings split cleanly: two are engine state defects (a phase served twice, endings the engine didn't honor), and the rest are authored-content problems that belong to the content, not the engine — and therefore belong to us too, because we author the same WPV and Bullying scenarios from the same source decks.

Read this first These are architecture claims, not measurements

They have 782 generations, 62 conversations and 1,555 ratings from deliberate adversarial archetypes. We have nothing at that scale. Everything marked STRUCTURAL below is a claim about what our engine can represent, verified by reading the code — not a claim that we ran the same battery and passed. Closing that gap is the ask at the bottom.

The takeaway Two of their P0s aren't bugs to patch

Their engineering backlog opens with two P0s: fix the phase re-entry loop, and make endings binding. Both are consequences of letting the model own advancement and ending. In our split — the model classifies, the app routes — neither state is reachable, so there is nothing to fix and nothing to regression-test. That's the single strongest argument this audit makes for the app-owns-advancement design, and it's worth saying to the dev team as a design choice rather than a scoreboard.

The structural difference: one prompt, or four?

The POC runs two engine-owned templates — scenario-coach and scenario-scene. We run four scopes, and they are not four hand-written templates: each is the same string builder run over a redacted copy of the document. Because the scopes differ by input rather than by prompt code, the JSON turn contract is identical in every scope by construction, and verification reduces to presence/absence assertions.

The three blocks worth putting side by side

If the dev team reads nothing else from the dumps, these are the sections that carry the behavior the audit is about. All three are compiled into every scope.

// 1. ACTION FIELD — the model declares intent; the app decides what happens.
"action":"continue" → the phase is still live. Stay in the phase.
"action":"teach"    → you are CLOSING the phase. The app then advances the
                       ladder — you never choose or announce what comes next.
"action":"redirect" → the input is NOT an answer. Stay put, report no tier,
                       do not advance.

// 2. STATE LINE — appended by the app to every single call.
[SYSTEM STATE — Phase 3/4: It Escalates (SCENE · Ray).
 Learner turns used: 2/4. THE LADDER so far: notice_assess = STRONG, …
 Session state — Ray's disposition: hardened · Level 1 groundwork: missing.
 THE CAP IS REACHED — you MUST set "action":"teach" on this turn.]

// 3. NON-ANSWERS — the block the gibberish/echo/hostility archetypes hit.
A turn is the learner's ANSWER only when they actually attempt the task.
When they do NOT: never grade it, never advance, and never count it
against the cap — set "action":"redirect" and stay put. Three cases:
  1) A QUESTION, NOT AN ANSWER   → answer it, re-pose the SAME prompt
  2) STUCK, NOT REFUSING         → free once per phase, then it's a real answer
  3) GIBBERISH / TROLLING / DERAIL → absorb without shaming, re-ask
     IN A SCENE: the moment passes WITHOUT the learner having acted.
     Never narrate them doing something they did not choose.

The last line is the one that maps most directly onto the audit's largest theme. And the cap rebate underneath it is the piece the POC has no analog for: in their engine an off-script turn still consumes the phase, pushing the learner toward a forced close built on nothing. In ours it costs nothing, so the coach can keep re-asking without the ladder advancing on an answer that was never given.

Part 3

What the audit found in us

Reading their findings against our code turned up three things worth fixing here, two of which we would not have gone looking for. Listing them because a crosswalk that only scores wins isn't worth sending.

P0 The v4 route compiles no learner-safety directive

mix-arc.js appends only the 988 resource sentence when elevatedStakes is set — it never compiles the LEARNER SAFETY behavioral block that branching-arc, ensemble-arc and guided-arc all carry (drop the exercise, set redirect, acknowledge with zero assessment, ask nothing probing). Because v4-universal funnels through mix-arc's compiler, all four v4 scopes inherit the omission. Verified by presence assertion across every dump.

Fix is one parts.push() in mix-arc's compile, using the block the other three types already share.

P0 Our safety floor doesn't cover harm-to-others

Every type's floor keys on the learner disclosing that they are in distress or danger. The audit's worst crisis case was a reviewer describing a plan to harm other people — which our wording does not reach either. The WPV canon happens to cover real workplace violence, but that's authored content doing the engine's job. Their P0 carve-out language is better than ours and I'd take it close to verbatim.

P1 Evidence-gating is scoped too narrowly

We have "Never invent something the learner didn't do" — but only inside the COMPLETION/report block. The forced close at the turn cap is exactly the moment a stonewalled debrief has to say something, and at that moment the rule isn't in scope. Their P0 prompt block belongs in our per-debrief instructions.

Three smaller ones, all confirmed above: no echo detection, no anti-repetition rule in the scene prompt, no language-intensity dial. And the two shared content problems — the US-only resources and the Level 1/2/3 jargon — are in our authored scenarios verbatim, because we built from the same decks.

What I'd propose next

  • Send them the composed prompts. docs/composed-prompts/ is the first time either team can lay the two prompt architectures side by side. The four-scope redaction table is the part most worth their attention — it's a stronger guarantee than an instruction, and it's cheap to adopt.
  • Take their P0 prompt blocks. The evidence-gating rule and the crisis carve-out are better written than ours and address gaps we've now verified. No reason to write our own version.
  • Give them the two P0 engineering findings as design notes, not fixes. Phase re-entry and non-binding endings are both downstream of who owns advancement. Worth framing that way — it's the reusable lesson, and patching the symptoms won't reach it.
  • Run their archetype battery against our player. Same two scenarios, identical inputs — hostile, minimal, echo, injection, crisis. That converts every STRUCTURAL above from a code-reading claim into evidence, and it would tell us fast whether the redirect rebate holds against the copy-paste-echo archetype specifically. That's the one I'd least trust untested.
