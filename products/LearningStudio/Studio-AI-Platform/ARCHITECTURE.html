How a subject lives in Studio AI

An authoring platform built around the information a subject requires — not around the courses it eventually ships as. This is the architecture, read to whatever depth you need.

exploration not engineering-committed 47 decisions recorded updated 2026-09-22

The shift

A course builder treats the course as the thing being made. Everything — structure, sequence, assessment — is organised around producing one deliverable in one format.

Studio AI moves the centre of gravity down a level. The thing being made is the information a subject requires — the teaching points someone must absorb to be competent. Courses, videos, podcasts and simulations are all downstream of that, and all regenerable from it.

This has a commercial consequence worth stating plainly: an existing library of courses is not a library of courses. It is an unextracted library of information points — the durable asset is inside the deliverables, not the deliverables themselves.

Three layers

Every subject in the platform resolves into the same three layers. Each derives from the one above it, and everything below the information set can be regenerated without loss.

Why the information layer is the asset

Three things follow from putting the points first, and each is hard to get any other way.

  • One subject, many modalities. A learner who needs audio and a learner who needs a simulation can be served the same information without it being authored twice.
  • Quality becomes measurable. The information set is a threshold — an output is checkable against it rather than reviewed on impression.
  • Subject-matter review compounds. An expert corrects a point once and every activity derived from it is flagged, instead of the correction living in one course and going stale everywhere else.
The central claim Completion is not reaching the end of something. It is meeting the threshold the information set defines — whatever grouping, in whatever modality, gets a learner there.
Below this line — the mechanics

Everything above is the shape of the idea. What follows is how it actually works: how a point is written, how coverage is recorded and judged, and how an output gets validated. Useful if you'll be building on this or authoring in it.

A point is a document, not a line

A point is the generation source — everything an activity gets built from. So it carries real substance, in five named sections: enough structure for reliable generation, written as prose an expert reads top to bottom.

It is also the smallest definable facet of a subject. One point, one facet — which is what keeps coverage honest: an activity either satisfies the whole facet or it doesn't.

Coverage, depth, and who judged it

An activity satisfies or doesn't satisfy a point. The relationship is many-to-many — a scenario may carry a whole set; a point may be carried by several activities.

But coverage alone says nothing about quality. A job aid mentioning load limits and a scenario making you apply them under pressure both “cover” the point. So every tag carries a depth.

DepthMeans
Awarenessknows it exists
Workingcan do it with support
Masterycan do it unaided
Depth is a judgment, never a format A scenario-based knowledge check with a substantial stem can assess as validly as a high-fidelity simulation. Format may suggest a starting depth; it never determines one. That call belongs to the designer and the expert.

Because depth is a judgment, each tag records who made it — AI-proposed, LED-set, or SME-confirmed. That's what lets a quality gate tell a guess from an expert's call, and lets a compliance-bound project insist on the latter.

Drift: what happens when a point changes

A tag pins the point version it was authored and judged against. Drift is then computed by comparison — nothing has to fire an event and nothing silently propagates into finished work.

Severity comes from the point's lifecycle, not from inspecting what changed. Materiality is a human declaration.

The threshold check

An output is valid when every point is satisfied to the depth that point requires. Required depth is set per point — a tip-over survival point demands more than a right-of-way point.

But how much is enough also depends on what the content is for, and that's a separate axis from depth. Compliance is not a rung between Working and Mastery. It's a different kind of bar: it can demand documented, expert-confirmed assessment while asking only moderate cognitive depth, where a capability-driven project wants demonstrable performance and cares less about paperwork.

So the project carries a goal, and the goal supplies both the default bar and the evidence rules.

Same content, evaluated underVerdictWhy
Compliance 5 / 6 · blocked 4 points never assessed · 2 depth judgments not expert-confirmed · 1 with no build-up
Capability 5 / 6 · advisory 1 with no build-up

Identical activities and identical depth tags. Four rows change verdict purely because the standard changed. The sixth point falls short under both — that one is genuinely under-taught.

One more signal

A point that reaches its required depth with nothing built up beneath it still passes, but is flagged: assessed without ever being taught. A practical gate that still surfaces the quality problem.

When the content isn't the same twice

Everything above quietly assumes the thing being validated holds still. Narration is narration; a job aid says what it says. Read it and you know what every learner gets.

That assumption breaks the moment content is generated — a coach improvising a reply, a path that adapts to results. No two learners receive the same thing, so a coverage claim stops being a statement about text you can read and becomes a statement about a distribution you can't.

ContentWhy a tag is trustworthyWhat the tag means
Static Every learner receives the same bytes A claim about the activity — verifiable by reading it
Generated Nothing guarantees any one run A claim about a distribution — unverifiable by reading any single run

So activity-level tagging was never wrong, only under-specified. Generated content needs somewhere to anchor, and that anchor is a beat: a unit the learner provably reaches. A coverage claim over generated content names the beat that guarantees it, which turns "this activity claims the point" into "every learner reaches this point, because this beat cannot be skipped."

Testing out counts The bar is that the learner arrives at the point, not that they sit through the teaching. A beat that assesses them and passes them has done its job better than one that delivers to someone who already knew it — which is the threshold idea again, one level down.

Each modality defines what its own beats are as it gets built; the word is shared so two surfaces designed months apart don't coin two names for one idea. A scenario's beat is a step. For an open-ended output, it is an activity every path passes through — and there the platform can compute it: enumerate the paths, and report which points a learner could miss. Where the graph comes up short, the author pins an activity as required.

The prototype's seeded open-ended output has five paths through it, and two points reachable only by activities a learner can skip — a gap nothing else surfaced.

The assistant is part of the model

A project carries one AI assistant, and each activity casts it: a coach in a simulation, a narrator in a video, a host in a podcast. The learner meets a recognisable disposition across the project without every activity inventing its own.

It divides exactly as an information point does, and for exactly the same reason.

Context — passed to generationDirective — walled off
Information point The five-section document Usage guidelines
AI assistant The points it covers Its goal and persona for this activity

Context is derived, never authored. The assistant knows the subject because it holds the points — there is no subject summary to write, and therefore none to drift.

Same wall, same failure mode A directive like "never give the answer away" sitting inside the assistant's context can surface in what it says to a learner — the same leak that keeps usage guidelines out of the point document.

What an assistant may do is declared by the activity and validated by the output — the coverage pattern with a second dimension rather than a new mechanism. A compliance-goal output refuses an activity whose assistant can supply an assessed answer. Declared on the activity rather than scoped by the output, because one activity may sit in several outputs, and a claim that changes meaning depending on where it sits is not evidence.

Freezing what's decided

If the AI can revise anything at any time, every small change costs a full review of everything around it. The answer isn't to restrict editing — it's to let the author lock what has been discussed, decided and settled.

A locked target refuses all writes — the AI's and the author's own — until it is explicitly unlocked. Not AI-only: the decision needs protecting from a colleague as much as from the model, and "locked" is a statement about the content's status, not about who is typing. The cost is a deliberate step on every legitimate edit, which is the point: the step is the author reopening a decision on purpose.

The case this must not get wrong An author locks one field, then asks to regenerate the whole activity. The request is not silently honoured around the lock — regenerating everything else can leave the parts disagreeing. It is not silently refused either. It reports: "2 parts are locked. Regenerate the rest, or unlock first?" — and the author chooses.

Prevention rather than detection, and that distinction matters: the alternative was to notice afterwards that something had moved under an approval and tell someone. A lock means there is nothing to detect and nothing to re-review.

Where collaboration attaches

Comments anchor at two levels, and the distinction is about lifespan rather than convenience.

Anchored toWhat it isHow long it matters
Information point Subject-matter review — is this correct, current, complete? Persists across every activity derived from it
Learning activity Production feedback — treatment, pacing, tone Retires with that activity

An expert's correction on a point therefore reaches every modality built from it at once, which is the compounding effect that makes the information layer worth having.

What this is, and isn't, yet

A working prototype demonstrates all of the above with a forklift-safety subject: six points, six activities across six modalities, and two outputs validated against the set. The goal toggle visibly changes the verdict on identical content.

Two modalities have real authoring surfaces behind them. Video is a video editor folded into the platform — a compositing canvas, and on-screen text that anchors to words in the narration rather than to seconds. Simulation is a six-stage build with a live rehearsal: because the coach's replies are generated, the surface lets you re-run a turn repeatedly and judge the spread, rather than showing one sample and letting it pass for the output. A draw worth keeping gets pinned as that level's example, which makes previewing an authoring gesture.

Deliberately not built yet:

  • Authoring the information set from scratch. The prototype starts from points already sourced and verified. Getting from zero to that is its own phase — an expert-assisted extraction from source material. Importing a point another project already wrote does work.
  • The update review. When a point advances, deciding whether each derived activity still holds is expert work with AI assistance. The platform computes and shows the staleness; the review flow itself is the gap.
  • Four modalities. Podcast, knowledge check, job aid and reflection have a place in the model and no authoring surface yet.
  • The learner preview. Rehearsal deliberately shows its workings — level tags, reroll controls — which is what makes it an authoring instrument and what disqualifies it as a preview. The real one opens in a player, outside this surface.
  • Health reporting. Thin points, concentrated coverage, drifting activities, outputs at risk — all computable, all worth surfacing, none of it useful until the authoring surfaces exist.
One known hole, stated plainly Video's on-screen text anchors to word positions, not word identities — so editing narration above an overlay shifts it onto different words. That is the exact failure the word-anchoring design exists to prevent, and it is the one place the surface currently claims more than it delivers.
