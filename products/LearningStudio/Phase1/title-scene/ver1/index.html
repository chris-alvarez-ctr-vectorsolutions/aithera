Today, even with volumes of solid medical studies, mold and potential health effects related to mold are still controversial. Think about this situation, an office in southern Florida after a hurricane. Contractors had removed the paneling, and extensive mold could be seen on the underlying sheetrock. The building owner's insurance company questioned the necessity of removing the paneling so that the mold-infested sheetrock could be physically removed and replaced. They argued that many products and systems were available that claimed to kill mold in place, saving the cost of removal. Why not use one of those approaches instead of the hassle and expense of pulling off both the siding and having to reinstall it later and replacing all the moldy underlayer with new material? The more profound question was, even if some process could kill the mold in place behind the paneling, how could it be removed? Could working in an office with that much mold behind the walls make the occupants sick? If someone replied no, people would not get sick after the treatment for the mold, then the next question would be, could they prove it?
