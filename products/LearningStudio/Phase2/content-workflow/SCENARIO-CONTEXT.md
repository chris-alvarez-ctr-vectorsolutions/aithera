## 1. The learner's role & point of view

The learner is a **co-worker** — a peer of Marshall's, **not** a manager, HR representative, or
the target of the harassment.

- They have **worked alongside Marshall for ~8 months**.
- They have **personally witnessed most of the day-to-day incidents**.
- They are **uncertain what to call what they've seen, and unsure what their role is.** This
  uncertainty is the learner's starting emotional state and the crux of the scenario.
- Everything is authored in the **second person ("you")**, from this witness perspective.

**Design implication:** the scenario is about *bystander recognition and response*, not about
being the victim or the investigator. Scenes should keep the learner in the observer/peer seat
and surface decisions a co-worker realistically faces (notice → name it → decide whether/how to
act → who to tell).

## 2. The situation (canonical narrative)

Preserve this framing text as the scenario's foundational setup. Downstream copy may be
tightened or split across scenes, but must not contradict these facts.

> You've been working alongside **Marshall** for about eight months. He's an **administrative
> assistant** — organized, a good communicator, clearly someone who takes his job seriously.
> But lately, he's not himself.
>
> It started with **Ethan**, the project manager. He'd greet Marshall with **"Hey Marsha!"** in
> the hallway. A couple of times he asked if Marshall had **a skirt on "under that desk."**
> Marshall let it go. He thought some joking might come with the job — especially given the way
> he dresses. So he tried not to make it a thing.
>
> Then **Jake** started. A junior engineer, hired not long after Marshall. He'd ask **if the
> coffee was made** whenever he passed Marshall's desk. He'd refer to Marshall's role as a
> **"cozy lady job."** What started as occasional became almost daily — the kind of remark that
> gets a few laughs and then everyone moves on. Except Marshall doesn't move on. He carries it.
>
> What Marshall didn't know, not at first, was that there was a **group chat**. Someone
> eventually showed him: sexist memes, jokes. And **two altered images** — one with his face on
> a woman in a frilly princess dress, another with his face on a lingerie model's body,
> captioned **"Marsha's true calling."**
>
> He was going to try to let it go. Until **those images ended up on public social media** —
> shareable, commentable, out there.
>
> You've seen most of the day-to-day. Marshall has **gotten quieter** — he keeps his head down,
> doesn't linger. You're not sure what to call any of it, or what your role is.

## 3. Cast

| Character | Role | Relationship to learner | Function in the scenario |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Marshall** | Administrative assistant, ~8 months in role. Organized, strong communicator, takes the job seriously. Dresses in a way that has been used as a pretext for the jokes. | The colleague the learner has worked alongside; the **target** of the harassment. | The person being harmed. Increasingly withdrawn. Not present as a "quest-giver" — the learner observes his decline. |
| **Ethan** | Project manager (relatively senior). | Coworker; more senior than the learner. | **First aggressor.** Verbal, "plausibly joking" harassment. His seniority raises the stakes of speaking up. |
| **Jake** | Junior engineer, hired shortly after Marshall. | Coworker; peer/junior. | **Second aggressor.** Escalates frequency (occasional → near-daily) and adds gendered belittling of the role. |
| **The learner ("you")** | Co-worker / peer of Marshall. | — | The **bystander** making sense of what they've witnessed and deciding what to do. |
| **The group chat participants** | Unnamed coworkers. | Coworkers. | The wider culture — laughter, memes, and the altered images. Represents diffusion of responsibility and how "jokes" scale. |

> **Naming note:** "Marsha" is the derogatory nickname used *against* Marshall. Use it only when
> voicing an aggressor or quoting an incident — never as narration.

## 4. Timeline / escalation arc

The incidents escalate along two axes — **severity** and **reach** — which is useful for
sequencing scenes and for showing why "letting it go" stops being viable.

1. **Verbal, framed as joking (Ethan).** "Hey Marsha!"; asking about a skirt "under that desk."
   Marshall rationalizes it as part of the job and stays quiet.
2. **Frequency ramps, role belittled (Jake).** "Is the coffee made?"; a "cozy lady job."
   Occasional becomes almost daily; the group laughs and moves on.
3. **Coordinated & documented (group chat).** Sexist memes and jokes in a chat Marshall wasn't
   part of. Two **altered images** (princess dress; lingerie body) captioned "Marsha's true
   calling."
4. **Public exposure (social media).** The altered images are posted publicly — shareable,
   commentable. This is the tipping point that removes any "it's just harmless joking" reading.
5. **Visible impact.** Marshall withdraws — quieter, head down, doesn't linger. The learner
   notices the change without a clean label for the cause.

**Design implication:** each rung is a natural candidate for a scene or decision beat. Early
rungs test *recognition* ("is this a problem?"); later rungs test *response* ("it's escalated
and gone public — now what?").

## 5. Themes & learning-relevant beats

Scenes and knowledge checks can draw on these without the document prescribing exact learning
objectives (those get set during scenario configuration):

- **"Just joking" as cover.** How harassment is normalized when it's framed as humor and gets
  laughs. The learner's own uncertainty ("some joking might come with the job") mirrors
  Marshall's.
- **Gender-based harassment.** The conduct targets Marshall with feminizing insults; the theme
  is gender-based harassment regardless of anyone's actual identity.
- **Escalation & inaction.** Silence and laughter let each rung enable the next.
- **Digital / off-channel conduct.** The group chat and public posts show harassment extends
  beyond face-to-face and beyond work hours/premises.
- **The bystander's dilemma.** Power dynamics (Ethan is senior), social risk, and "not my
  business" pull against acting. The scenario's core question: *what is a co-worker's role?*
- **Recognizing impact.** Reading behavioral change (withdrawal) as a signal.

## 6. Facts to keep consistent (quick-reference for scene authoring)

Use this as a checklist so generated scenes don't drift:

- Learner = **co-worker/peer**, **~8 months** alongside Marshall, **has witnessed** the day-to-day.
- Marshall = **administrative assistant**, **~8 months** in role.
- Ethan = **project manager**, first aggressor, **verbal** ("Hey Marsha!", skirt "under that desk").
- Jake = **junior engineer**, hired **after** Marshall, second aggressor ("coffee made?", "cozy lady job"), **near-daily**.
- **Group chat** = memes + **two altered images** (princess dress; lingerie body), caption **"Marsha's true calling."**
- **Public social media** exposure of the altered images = the **tipping point**.
- Marshall's response over time = **withdrawal** (quieter, head down, doesn't linger).
- Point of view = **second person**; the learner does **not** yet know "what to call it" or "what their role is."
